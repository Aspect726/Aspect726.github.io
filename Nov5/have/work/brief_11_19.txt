S14. Sunday, Dec 20. Polygons, nets, and surface area
•
Standards. 6.G.A.1, 6.G.A.4.
•
Goals. Find the area of a composite polygon by decomposing it or by subtracting. Draw and read nets of
prisms and pyramids. Treat surface area as the sum of the areas of all faces. Tell square units from cubic
units.
•
Cases. An L-shaped polygon split two ways. A rectangular prism net (3 × 4 × 5 box, 94 square units). A
triangular prism net (two triangles and three rectangles). A square pyramid net (one square and four
triangles). A surface-area calculation with one face missing, for the student to find the error.
•
Misconceptions. Leaving out hidden faces. Using the volume formula. Wrong units. Forgetting the 1/2 on
triangular faces.
•
Exit ticket target. A triangular prism whose triangle has sides 3, 4, 5 cm and whose length is 10 cm (surface
area 132 square cm).
S15. Saturday, Dec 26. Negative numbers: opposites, order, absolute value
•
Standards. 6.NS.C.5 to 6.NS.C.7.
•
Goals. Use positive and negative numbers for temperature, elevation, and money. Locate them on a number
line. Understand opposites. Understand absolute value as distance from zero. Order and compare rational
numbers, including fractions and decimals. Interpret statements such as −8 < −3.
•
Cases. Contexts (below zero, below sea level, owes money). Integers on a number line. Fractions and
decimals on a number line (−3.5, −1/2). The opposite of −7 and the opposite of the opposite. Absolute value
of positive and negative numbers. Comparing numbers versus comparing absolute values (−7 and −4).
Real-world statements.
•
Misconceptions. −8 > −3 because 8 > 3. Absolute value means "drop the sign" with no sense of distance.
−(−5) = −5.
•
Exit ticket target. Order −5, 2, −1/2, −3.5 from least to greatest, and name which has the greatest absolute
value (−5, −3.5, −1/2, 2; greatest absolute value is −5).
S16. Sunday, Dec 27. The coordinate plane and inequalities
•
Standards. 6.NS.C.6, 6.NS.C.8, 6.EE.B.5, 6.EE.B.8.
•
Timing. Coordinate plane 35 minutes. Inequalities 25 minutes. Teach GCF and LCM in the weekday
packets and study guide this week, not in the session.
•
Goals. Plot and read points in all four quadrants. Reflect points across the axes. Find distances between
points on the same horizontal or vertical line. Write and graph inequalities on a number line.
•
Cases. Points in each quadrant. Coordinates read from a graph. Reflections of (−3, 2) across each axis.
Distance between (−3, 2) and (−3, −5). A polygon on the plane with its perimeter. Open and closed circles (x
> 3 and x ≥ 3). Whether 3 is a solution of x > 3. Writing an inequality from words ("at least 12").
•
Misconceptions. Swapping x and y. Sign errors by quadrant. Reflecting across the wrong axis. Confusing
"at least" with "more than."
•
Exit ticket target. Reflect A(−3, 2) across the y-axis and give the distance from A to its image (A′ = (3, 2),
distance 6).

S17. Saturday, Jan 2. Data: statistical questions, displays, mean and median
•
Standards. 6.SP.A.1 to 6.SP.A.3, 6.SP.B.4, 6.SP.B.5.
•
Goals. Recognize a statistical question. Build and read dot plots and histograms. Describe center: the mean
as a fair share and as a balance point, and the median as the middle. Describe the shape of a distribution.
Choose a measure of center.
•
Cases. Statistical versus non-statistical questions. A dot plot from data. A histogram with given bins. The
mean of a small data set. A median with an odd number of values. A median with an even number of values.
The effect of an outlier on the mean versus the median. A missing value that produces a given mean.
•
Misconceptions. Finding the median without ordering the data. Forgetting to average the two middle values.
Mixing up mean and most common value. Confusing a histogram with a bar graph.
•
Exit ticket target. Data 7, 3, 9, 4, 12, 4: find the median and the mean (median 5.5, mean 6.5).
S18. Sunday, Jan 3. Variability: range, MAD, IQR, box plots. Includes the Grade 6 Cumulative
Check
•
Standards. 6.SP.A.2, 6.SP.A.3, 6.SP.B.5.
•
Timing. Lesson 40 minutes, Grade 6 Cumulative Check 30 minutes.
•
Goals. Measure spread with the range, the IQR, and the MAD. Build and read box plots from a five-number
summary. Compare two data sets using center and spread.
•
Cases. Range. The five-number summary for an odd and for an even number of values. IQR. MAD

Exit ticket target. Data 2, 4, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12: find Q1, the median, Q3, and the IQR (4, 6, 9, 5).

S19. Saturday, Jan 9. Bridge to the rest of IMT1: scale drawings and proportional relationships
•
Standards. 7.G.A.1, 7.RP.A.1, 7.RP.A.2.
•
Goals. Understand scaled copies (all lengths multiplied by a scale factor, angles unchanged, area multiplied
by the square of the factor). Read map and drawing scales and find actual lengths. Understand a proportional
relationship through its constant of proportionality k = y/x, the equation y = kx, tables, and graphs through
the origin. Tell proportional from non-proportional relationships.
•
Cases. A whole-number scale factor. A fractional scale factor. A map scale with units (1 cm to 50 km). The
area of a scaled copy (factor 3 makes the area 9 times as large). A proportional table where k is the unit rate.
A non-proportional relationship (a taxi fare of $3 plus $2 per mile). A graph through the origin and one that
is not. An equation from a story.
•
Misconceptions. Applying the scale factor to area. Additive scaling. Treating a linear but non-proportional
relationship as proportional. Reversing x and y when computing k.
•
Exit ticket target. A table with points (2, 5), (4, 9), (6, 13): is it proportional? (No. It is linear, y = 2x + 1,
but not through the origin. A student who notices the constant difference may wrongly say yes.)

Part 5. How to design the lessons and practice
These rules apply to every slide, handout, packet, and study guide. They come from research on how children
learn math and from standard lesson-design practice. Follow them, and where you can improve on them for this
student, say so in the Design Decisions note.
5.1 Shape of a Saturday session (about 75 minutes)
1.
Retrieval warm-up (7 min). Four or five closed-book questions on earlier weeks, answered from memory,
then checked right away with feedback. Spacing and self-testing are among the best-supported learning
techniques.
2.
Launch (8 min). One problem he can start before any teaching, with no hints. The tutor asks what he
notices and what he would try.
3.
Explore (20 min). Two or three tasks using concrete or visual models. The tutor mostly asks questions
("How do you know?", "Can you draw it another way?") and does not demonstrate procedures first.
4.
Discuss (15 min). He explains, and the tutor and student compare two methods or agree or disagree with a
planted worked solution that contains an error. Use at least one named talk move (turn-and-talk with a
sibling, partner compare, agree or disagree). Write specific prompts for each discussion, never generic ones
such as "What do you think?"
5.
Synthesize (7 min). He completes a one- or two-sentence summary in his own words, and the key idea
goes in his notebook.
6.
Exit ticket (8 min). One problem, always the structurally hardest case from the session's blueprint (see
5.3). The tutor sorts the response into Got it, Almost there, or Needs re-teaching using the explicit criteria
you write for that session.
7.
Wrap-up (5 min). A preview of the week's packets, and any question he has.
That is 70 minutes with 5 minutes of slack. Sunday sessions (60 to 75 minutes) follow the same shape but
compress the warm-up to 5 minutes, put 10 minutes of interleaved mixed review (drawn from the last three
weeks) before the exit ticket, and allow 12 minutes for Discuss. Exact timings for the diagnostic, S16, S18, and
S20 are in Part 4. Add up the timings on every session plan and make sure they fit.
5.2 Concrete, then pictorial, then abstract
Introduce an idea with objects or a drawing, then connect it to a diagram, and only then to a rule or algorithm.
Do not show an algorithm (invert and multiply, cross-multiply, "move the decimal") before the model that
explains it. The models for this program are number lines, fraction strips, tape diagrams, double number lines,
ratio tables, area models, base-ten diagrams, hanger diagrams, nets, and the coordinate plane. Use the same
model, drawn the same way, in the slides, the handouts, and the study guide.
5.3 Problem sets must have structural variety
Before writing the practice problems for a topic, list the structural cases of the skill from the baseline case (the
one every student must clear) to the hardest case (the one students most often get wrong). Then make sure every
case appears as a numbered, required problem. Rules:
•
A case mentioned only in prose does not count as covered. If the plan's text names a case, a numbered
problem must present it.

•
Emphasizing the hardest case never allows you to drop the baseline case. A set of only hard problems fails
coverage exactly as a set of only easy ones does.
•
The number domain is part of the variety: if the standard involves rational numbers, at least one required
problem uses fractions or decimals.
•
Never put a required case in an optional extension or bonus.
•
For every session, produce a coverage table (case to problem number) in the tutor run sheet so a gap is
visible at a glance.
5.4 Exit tickets catch the misconception
The exit ticket is the hardest case from the blueprint. Apply the misconception test: a student who holds the
session's main misconception must get the exit ticket wrong. If that student would get it right, you picked a case
that merely affirms the idea, so swap it for one that discriminates. Verify each exit ticket and its answer by
actually working the problem in code. The operation count must match the standard.
5.5 Error analysis in every session
Each session includes a planted wrong solution from a named student (use ordinary, varied first names) and
asks him to find the mistake, explain why it happens, and fix it. The wrong solutions must come from the real
misconceptions listed in the blueprints, not from random slips.
5.6 Retrieval, spacing, interleaving
•
Daily practice should mix topics. Do not group a packet by one topic only.
•
Use the spiral rules in Part 7.
•
Prefer closed-book recall with feedback to rereading, highlighting, or summarizing.
•
Interleaved practice helps most after some blocked practice has built the basic idea, so a new topic first
appears in a blocked set (Saturday and Sunday sheets, Monday and Tuesday packets) and then moves into
mixed sets.
5.7 Worked examples that fade
Alternate worked examples with problems to solve. Early in a topic, show a complete worked example and then
a similar problem. Later, show a partly worked example for him to finish ("complete the worked example").
Annotate the thinking behind each step, not only the steps.
5.8 Explaining and drawing
Ask for one or two "explain it" or "draw it" items per packet, and at most two per session sheet. A sentence
frame is allowed only when writing the explanation is itself the hard part of the problem. Self-explanation takes
longer, so do not overuse it. He should do most of the explaining and drawing, and the tutor should not
demonstrate procedures before he has tried.
5.9 Language
•
Define each math term in plain words the first time it appears, with an example, and use the same term every
time afterward in slides, packets, and guides.

•
Typical sentence length is 20 words or fewer. Contexts must be familiar to a 9 or 10 year old (food, sports,
games, pets, maps, money, building things). Avoid trivia that needs special background. Vary names. Avoid
violent contexts.
•
Never label a problem "easy" or "hard" in student text. Avoid "just" ("just multiply").
5.10 Fluency
Short fluency work is part of every weekday packet (the warm-up), but accuracy comes before speed, and no
timer is ever used to pressure him. A timed multiplication-fact check can appear in the diagnostic and in Mini
Check A and the Friday checks of weeks 3, 6, and 9, framed as "beat your own time."
5.11 Gifted considerations
He needs depth and interesting problems, not only speed. Include one original brain teaser per week
(Wednesday packet), a few clearly marked optional Stretch problems, and "why does this work?" questions. Do
not use acceleration as a reason to skip understanding.
5.12 Realistic timing
Do not overload sessions or packets. A computation problem takes about 1 to 2 minutes, a multi-step word
problem 3 to 5 minutes, an explain item 2 to 3 minutes. Count the items and check each packet against 15 to 20
minutes. Check each session against its stated minutes.

Part 6. The slide deck
6.1 What to deliver
One PowerPoint file named DoubleAdvanced_Weekend_Slides.pptx that contains all 20 sessions in order. It is
used on a TV or laptop, with the tutor and student side by side. The deck must open correctly in PowerPoint,
Keynote, and Google Slides.
•
16:9, 13.333 by 7.5 inches.
•
Front matter: slide 1 is a plain title slide. Slide 2 is "How a session runs" (the routine from 5.1 in kid-friendly
words). Slide 3 is the map of all 20 sessions with dates.
•
Each session has 16 to 20 slides, except S1 (about 10 to 12, because the diagnostic is on paper), and S18 and
S20 (about 16, because part of the session is a paper check or mixed review). The total will be roughly 330
to 380 slides. That is intended.
•
Each session begins with a title slide ("Week 3, Saturday. Rates and unit rates") and ends with a hidden tutor
slide holding the answers, the timing map, and the exit-ticket sort criteria. Mark that slide hidden so it does
not appear in slideshow mode.
•
Speaker notes on every slide (see 6.5).
•
No animations, transitions, audio, or video. Where something must be revealed step by step, use consecutive
slides ("build slides") that add one element each.
•
File size under 30 MB. Build all diagrams from native shapes, lines, and tables, not from images, so they
stay sharp and editable.
6.2 Slide order inside a session
1.
Session title slide.
2.
Today: one or two sentences of the goal in student words, and the agenda with minutes.
3.
Retrieval warm-up questions (one slide with 4 or 5 numbered questions), then the answers slide.
4.
Launch problem (one problem, plenty of white space), plus a "notice and wonder" prompt slide if useful.
5.
Explore tasks, 3 to 5 slides: the task, then the model that helps, built up in steps.
6.
Worked example slides, 2 or 3: "Watch the thinking" then "Your turn."
7.
Discuss, 2 or 3 slides: a comparison of two methods, the error-analysis slide, and the specific discussion
prompts.
8.
Vocabulary or key idea slide, with a plain definition and an example.
9.
Synthesize: a sentence frame for his own summary.
10.
Exit ticket: one problem, large, with room to work.
11.
Preview: what the week's packets will practice.
12.
Hidden tutor slide.
Mix the layouts. A deck where every slide looks the same reads as machine-made.

6.3 Layout catalogue
Define a small set of layouts and reuse them with care: title, goal and agenda, quiz list, single problem, model
with caption, worked example (steps on the left, diagram on the right), compare (two methods side by side),
error analysis ("Sam wrote this. What went wrong?"), vocabulary, sentence frame, exit ticket, preview, and
tutor.
6.4 Design: it must not look like an AI template
The design should look like it was made by a thoughtful teacher with good taste. Quiet, readable, and a little
old-fashioned in a good way.
Do.
•
Left-align text. Put titles at the top left. Use one consistent grid and generous margins (0.7 inch).
•
Use a warm white background (#FAF7F0) or plain white, near-black text (#1F1F1F), one primary accent
(deep teal #1F5A63), one secondary accent (muted brick #A3472B), and a mid-gray (#6B6B6B) for footers.
Use a soft yellow (#F2D46B) only to highlight a number or a phrase.
•
Use at most two typefaces. Georgia for titles. Calibri for body text. Do math expressions in Georgia or
Cambria Math so numerals and fractions look clean. These fonts are safe across PowerPoint, Keynote, and
Google Slides.
•
Body text at least 26 point (28 preferred), titles 34 to 40 point, diagram labels at least 20 point. No more than
about 40 words of body text per slide, not counting math.
•
One idea per slide. Let white space do work.
•
Use color to mean something. Quantity A is always teal, quantity B always brick, and a correct or
highlighted result may use the soft yellow. Never rely on color alone: also label.
•
Draw diagrams precisely and to scale: evenly spaced tick marks on number lines, aligned rectangles for tape
diagrams, true right angles, labels placed clear of lines. Use line widths of 2 to 3 point.
•
Put a small footer in gray at the bottom left ("Week 3 · Saturday · Rates") and the slide number at the bottom
right.
Do not.
•
No stock photos, clip art, emoji, icons, cartoon mascots, or decorative shapes.
•
No gradients, drop shadows, glow, 3D effects, or semi-transparent overlays.
•
No purple-to-blue or neon palettes. No glassy cards. No grids of rounded-corner cards with icons. No hero
numbers. No underline bar under every title. No centered, all-caps titles.
•
No walls of bold text. No more than one bold phrase per slide.
•
No slide that is only a title with a decorative background.
Render the whole deck to images and look at it (see Part 12). If a slide looks like a default template, redo it.
6.5 Speaker notes
Every slide has notes the tutor can read at a glance, in this order and at most about 120 words (Discuss slides
may run longer):
1.
Say. A short script in plain spoken language.
2.
Ask. The exact questions to put to him.

3.
Expect. The answers or strategies he is likely to give, including common wrong ones.
4.
If stuck. One or two moves that help without giving the answer away.
5.
Time. How many minutes this slide should take.
6.6 Writing style for the slides
Write like a patient, competent tutor talking at a board. Plain, concrete, direct. Address the student as "you."
Short sentences. Statements instead of questions in titles.
•
Titles are plain noun phrases or statements: "Equivalent ratios," "Same ratio, different amounts," "Dividing
by a fraction."
•
No hype, no cheerleading, and no filler. Praise belongs in the tutor's mouth, not on the slide.
•
Avoid these phrases and patterns entirely: "Let's dive in," "Let's explore," "journey," "unlock," "delve,"
"embark," "In today's lesson we will," "Key takeaways," "Great job," "Awesome," "Ready to master,"
"power of," "game-changer," "not just X, but Y," three-item rhetorical lists, and rhetorical questions as titles.
•
No emoji. Use no more than two exclamation marks in an entire session.
•
Limit em dashes. In student-facing sentences, prefer periods and commas.
