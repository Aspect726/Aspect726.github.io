Part 7. Daily practice packets
7.1 What to deliver
50 packets: Monday to Friday after each of the ten weekends. Monday to Thursday are standard packets. Friday
is the weekly check. Each is a PDF. Provide one answer key per packet (see 7.6).
7.2 Format: plain, black and white, old-style type
The packets are meant to look like a classic printed worksheet. Plain and quiet.
•
US Letter, one sheet (a single page, or two pages front and back at most). Black text on white. No color, no
shading, no icons, no decorative borders, no boxes.
•
Typeface: an old-style serif. In order of preference: EB Garamond, a Palatino clone (TeX Gyre Pagella or
Palladio), Century Schoolbook (TeX Gyre Schola), then a Times clone (Liberation Serif or Times New
Roman) if nothing else is available. Never a sans serif. Embed the fonts in the PDF.
•
Body 12 to 13 point with 1.3 line spacing. Generous space to work after each problem (about 1.2 to 2 inches,
matched to the work the problem needs).
•
Header: a line "Name ______ Date ______" and, at the right, "Week 3, Day 2". A thin rule under the header
is allowed. A footer holds the packet ID, for example "W03D2".
•
Number the problems "1.", "2.", and so on. Put "Answer: ________" on problems that have one short
answer.
•
Math typesetting: stacked fractions in problems, mixed numbers with a stacked fraction (for example 2 and
1/4 set as a proper mixed number), × and ÷ for operations, a : b for ratios, a leading zero on decimals (0.5),
and consistent minus signs. Use a real typesetting route: LaTeX (preferred if available), or ReportLab or
WeasyPrint with a helper that draws stacked fractions properly. Do not write fractions as "3/4" on the
printed packet if you can set them stacked.
•
When a problem cannot be asked without a figure (a number line, a ruled table, a grid), draw it in plain black
line art, as small as is clear. Otherwise, use text only.
7.3 Structure of a Monday to Thursday packet (15 to 20 minutes)
•
Part A. Warm-up (4 items, 3 to 4 minutes). Fluency. Rotate the strand by day: Monday, multiplication and
division facts; Tuesday, fraction, decimal, and percent equivalents (1/8 = 0.125, 3/4 = 75%, and so on);
Wednesday, mental scaling and powers of 10 (times and divided by 10, 100, 1,000; doubling and halving);
Thursday, estimation.
•
Part B. Practice (8 to 10 items, 8 to 10 minutes). Mixed by the spiral rule below.
•
Part C. Think (1 or 2 items, 4 to 5 minutes). On Monday and Tuesday, one multi-step word problem or an
error-analysis item ("Maya says... What did she do wrong?"). On Wednesday, the weekly brain teaser (an
original puzzle that needs reasoning, not new content, suited to a bright 9 or 10 year old). On Thursday, a
word problem plus an explain item. In weeks 8 to 10 the Thursday packet adds a short section labeled
"Stretch (optional)" with 2 to 3 preview problems from IMT2's first units (signed numbers on a number line,
percent change, two-step equations).

7.5 Friday weekly check
•
Ten items, 20 minutes, no calculator, printed in the same plain format with the header "Weekly Check."
•
Mix: four items from the current weekend, three from the two weeks before, two from older weeks, and one
"explain" item.
•
Include a printed scoring guide in the key: 9 to 10 correct is strong, 7 to 8 is on track, 5 to 6 means repeat the
topic with a Booster set before moving on, and 4 or fewer means set aside 20 minutes of the next session for
a re-teach. The guide also maps each item to a skill ID so misses point to the right Booster set.
•
Mini Check A (week 3, Sunday), the Grade 6 Cumulative Check (week 9, Sunday), and the final mixed
review (week 10, Sunday) are paper checks in the same format.
7.6 Answer keys
For every packet, check, and handout, make a separate key and put it in the Tutor_Only folder, never in the
student's folder. Each key gives the final answer to every item, a short worked solution for multi-step items, and
for two or three items the most likely wrong answer and the error behind it. Also tell the tutor, for each packet,
which Booster set to use if he misses three or more items on the same topic.

Part 8. Weekend handouts, run sheets, and study guides
8.1 Weekend session sheets (student)
For each of the 20 sessions: a plain black-and-white sheet in the same old-style type as the packets, two pages at
most. It holds the launch problem and the explore tasks with working space, and the exit ticket on its own page
(so it can be collected). The exit ticket is a fresh problem, never a duplicate of a practice problem.
8.2 Tutor run sheets
For each of the 20 sessions: a one-page PDF in Tutor_Only with:
•
the timed agenda and materials list (name the items plainly, for example "paper fraction strips" and "grid
paper"),
•
the learning goal in one sentence, the key idea, and the prerequisite skill,
•
three or more look-fors during Explore, each written as: the student response, why it matters, and what to do
with it (and, if the task admits more than one correct approach, one look-for that says so, so the tutor accepts
all of them),
•
the misconception table: what students do, why it happens, the tutor's move (two or three rows),
•
specific discussion prompts and the named talk move,
•
the exit-ticket sort with full criteria for all three buckets: Got it, Almost there, Needs re-teaching. Each
bucket must say what a response in it contains. Do not use the bare labels alone.
•
the coverage table from 5.3.
8.3 Weekly study guide
One per week, 2 to 4 pages, in a clean textbook look. This is the one place where visual elements are welcome,
and you may use color. It must still print acceptably in grayscale, so never rely on color alone.
Include, in this order:
1.
The big idea in three sentences or fewer, plain language.
2.
Words to know. Each term with a kid-friendly meaning and a small example.
3.
How it works. The week's models drawn carefully (tape diagrams, number lines, area models, and so on)
with short annotations, matching the slides.
4.
Worked examples. Two or three, step by step, with the thinking beside each step.
5.
Common mistakes. Three entries: what it looks like, why it happens, how to fix it.
6.
Try it. Five problems with answers printed in a separate block at the end of the guide.
7.
Where this connects. One or two sentences: what it builds on and what comes next.
Use the same palette as the slides (teal and brick) for callout boxes and diagrams, light tinted boxes (#F3EFE6)
for definitions, and the same old-style serif for body text, with a clean bold style for headings. No clip art, no
gradients, no decorative icons.

Part 9. The diagnostic
Build a diagnostic for Saturday of week 1 (Week_01 folder), with a key and a scoring map. It should take about
35 minutes in two sittings with a short break. It is not a test he can fail. Its purpose is to show the tutor where the
gaps are.
Part A: computation, no calculator (about 15 minutes, around 15 items). A 2-minute multiplication-fact
check (about 30 mixed facts to 12 × 12, written in a scrambled order). 347 × 26. 1,248 ÷ 6. A division with a
2-digit divisor and a 4-digit dividend. Decimal addition and subtraction with different lengths (3.45 + 12.8, 7.2
− 3.56). A decimal product (0.6 × 0.8) and a decimal division. Fractions: 2/3 × 3/4, 3/4 + 1/6, "How many 1/3s
are in 2?", 1/4 ÷ 2, and a mixed number times a whole number. Compare 3/8 and 2/5.
Part B: reasoning (about 20 minutes, around 11 items). Place 2/3, 5/4, 7/8, and 1 1/2 on one number line.
Order 0.5, 0.45, and 0.405. A tape-diagram ratio problem. Find 25% of 48. Evaluate 3n + 2 for n = 5. A
negative-number temperature question in plain language. The area of a rectangle with fractional sides. The
volume of a box. Plot and read points in the first quadrant of the coordinate plane. "Explain why 1/2 × 6 is
smaller than 6" (the scaling idea). Two non-routine problems that test persistence rather than content (for
example a pattern problem and a logic problem), clearly marked as ones he may find hard.
Write the actual problems yourself. Tag each item with a skill ID (for example F-COMP for comparing
fractions) and produce:
•
an answer key,
•
a scoring map (skill by item, with a simple traffic-light rule such as at least 80% secure, 50% to 79%
developing, below 50% priority),
•
a "results to plan" table that tells the tutor what to do: which Booster sets to add to the weekday packets of
week 1, whether to slow week 2, and whether to swap a fluency strand. The packets are pre-built and cannot
change, so this table works through the Booster Library and through the tutor's choices, not through
regenerated files.

Part 11. How to work, in phases
Phase A. Plan, with no files yet. Read this whole document. Write a master blueprint for all 20 sessions: for
each, confirm the standards, the learning goal, the structural cases mapped to problem numbers, the
misconception and the exit ticket, the models, the slide list, and the packet themes for the five days that follow.
Write the Design Decisions note. Check the blueprint against Part 5. Do not begin building until the blueprint
passes your own check. Keep this phase tight: no long essays.
Phase B. Build tools. Write reusable code before content: a slide-builder with the layouts from 6.3 and diagram
functions (number line, tape diagram, double number line, ratio table, area model, hanger diagram, coordinate
plane, dot plot, histogram, box plot, net, triangle and parallelogram with height, circle). Write a packet-builder
that sets stacked fractions and old-style type correctly, a key-builder, and a math-verification helper (use exact
fractions, not floating point, for answers). Store each session's content as structured data (JSON) so everything
can be regenerated and corrected.
Phase C. Build Week 1 completely as a pilot. All files for Week 1, including the diagnostic and the first
slides. Run the full quality check (Part 12). Fix everything you find. Then continue.
Phase D. Build weeks 2 to 10, one week at a time. After each week, run the quality check, and post a
three-line status (what was built, what you fixed, what comes next). Do not stop to ask permission between
weeks. If your output limits force a stop, end with a clear note naming the next week to build, so the person can
reply "continue."
Phase E. Assemble and package. Build the single deck from all sessions' data, render it, and check it. Build the
Start Here folder, the Booster Library, and the Printables. Write the manifest. Create the ZIPs. Write the final
report (Part 13).
Your freedom. You may reorder topics within a week or move a topic across a weekend if you can say why.
You may change the number of problems, the exact numbers, and the contexts. You may change slide counts
within 14 to 20 per session. You may add Booster sets or printables you think are valuable. You may improve
any rule in this document if you explain why in Design Decisions.
What you may not change. The ten-week, 20-session structure. The one-deck rule. The plain black-and-white
old-style format for packets, session sheets, and keys. The rule that all content is original. The rule that answers
are verified with code. The priority order of topics in 3.3.

Part 12. Quality check (mandatory, before every delivery)
Math.
•
Every answer key value is produced or verified by code, using exact fractions and decimals. Where a
problem has more than one correct answer, the key says so.
•
No ambiguous wording. Units are correct. Numbers are friendly enough for a 9 or 10 year old to compute by
hand.
•
Geometry problems are possible: if a triangle has stated sides and a height, check them in code.
•
Each exit ticket passes the misconception test (5.4).
•
Each session has a coverage table showing every structural case in a numbered problem.
•
Notation is identical across slides, packets, guides, and keys.
Pedagogy.
•
Each session follows the shape in 5.1 and its timings add up. Discuss is at least 10 minutes on a Saturday.
•
Each session has an error-analysis item built on a real misconception.
•
Each packet fits 15 to 20 minutes by your item count and timing rules. Spiral percentages roughly follow
7.4.
Language.
•
Scan every slide, handout, and guide for the banned phrases in 6.6 and for emoji. Count exclamation marks
and em dashes.
•
Reading level matches a 9 or 10 year old, with math terms defined once and used the same way afterward.
Visual.
•
Render the deck (for example, convert to PDF with LibreOffice and then to images) and look at every slide.
Check for text overflow, overlap, crowding, and low contrast. Check every diagram for accurate spacing and
legible labels.
•
Render every PDF to images and look at it. Check page counts (packets are at most two pages), fonts
embedded, stacked fractions set properly, no clipped text, and no stray blank pages.
•
Confirm packets are black and white only and in an old-style serif.
Files.
•
Names and folders match Part 10. The manifest lists every file with its page or slide count. ZIP files open
and contain what they should.
If any check fails, fix the cause and regenerate. Do not deliver known problems.

Appendix A. Common misconceptions to design around
•
Fractions. A larger denominator means a larger fraction. Adding numerators and denominators. A fraction
must be less than 1. A fraction on a number line counted from the wrong unit.
•
Operations. Multiplication always makes a number bigger. Division always makes a number smaller.
Confusing 4 ÷ 1/3 with 1/3 ÷ 4.
•
Decimals. A decimal with more digits is larger. Aligning at the right edge instead of at the decimal point.
Misplacing the decimal point in a product or quotient.
•
Ratios. Additive instead of multiplicative reasoning. Comparing ratios by their difference. Confusing
part-to-part with part-to-whole.
•
Percent. Treating "x is 30% of a number" as "30% of x." Believing percent cannot exceed 100.
•
Algebra. A variable is one fixed number. 3² = 6. 3(x + 4) = 3x + 4. Different operations on the two sides of
an equation.
•
Negative numbers. −8 > −3 because 8 > 3. Absolute value as "drop the sign."
•
Geometry. Using the slanted side as the height. Forgetting the 1/2 in a triangle. Leaving out hidden faces in
surface area. Confusing area, surface area, and volume units.
•
Statistics. Median without ordering. Forgetting to average the middle two values. Splitting the range instead
of the data to find quartiles.
•
Proportional relationships. Treating a linear but non-proportional relationship as proportional. Applying a
scale factor to area.

Appendix B. Writing samples: weak and strong
Weak. Let's dive into the exciting world of ratios! A ratio is a powerful tool that lets us compare quantities in
amazing ways. Strong. A ratio compares two amounts. 3 red tiles for every 2 blue tiles is the ratio 3 : 2.
Weak. Great job! Now let's unlock the secrets of equivalent ratios! Strong. Try this before I show you
anything. A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 3 cups of water. How much flour goes with 9 cups of water?
Weak (title). Mastering Equivalent Ratios: Your Key to Success Strong (title). Equivalent ratios
Weak (error analysis). Oops! Sam made a little mistake. Can you spot it? Strong (error analysis). Sam says 2
: 3 is the same as 4 : 5, because he added 2 to each number. Is he right? Explain, and show a way to check.
Weak (summary). Remember, the key takeaway is that fractions are amazing! Strong (summary). Finish the
sentence in your own words: To divide by a fraction, I ask how many ___ fit in ___.
