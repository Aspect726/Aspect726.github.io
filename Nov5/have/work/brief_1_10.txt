What this document is. This is a complete work order. Read all of it before you produce anything. It describes
a ten-week math tutoring program for one student and tells you exactly what to build: one PowerPoint deck for
the weekend lessons, daily practice packets, weekly study guides, weekend handouts, answer keys, and
printables. The research, the teaching plan, the lesson-design rules, and the formatting rules are all here. Your
job is to build the program, and to improve on the plan wherever you can justify the change.
Who you are for this job. A senior curriculum designer who knows upper-elementary and middle-school math
deeply, knows how children learn it, and can also produce clean, correct, professionally formatted files with
code.
How to work.
•
Think before you build. Spend real effort on the blueprint (Part 11, Phase A) before you generate any file.
Plan, check the plan against the rules in this document, then build.
•
The person who gave you this document cannot answer questions quickly. Make reasonable decisions
yourself, write them in the Design Decisions note, and keep going. Ask a question only if you cannot create
files at all, or if two instructions in this document directly contradict each other and the contradiction blocks
the work.
•
Where this document gives a rule, follow it. Where it gives a goal, use your own judgment to find the best
way to reach it. You may change the plan when you can explain the change in two sentences.
•
You may not skip a session, shorten the ten weeks, or break the format rules in Parts 6 to 8.
•
Verify every piece of math with code. Never trust mental arithmetic for an answer key.
•
Write all problems, contexts, and explanations yourself. Do not copy text from any published curriculum.
What you need to be able to do. Run code, create PPTX and PDF files, render them to images to inspect them,

Part 1. Mission and context
The student. A 4th grader, age 9 or 10, in the Advanced Learning "Centered" program at Cherry Crest
Elementary in the Bellevue School District (BSD), Washington. In class this year he studies Grade 5 math
standards using the Illustrative Mathematics (IM) Grade 5 course. He is bright, and he needs to be taught for
understanding. He is still nine: sessions must be concrete, short, and engaging, and nothing should feel like a
pile of worksheets.
The goal. BSD allows a small number of Advanced Learning elementary students to be accelerated one more
year in math, beyond the standard one-year acceleration. The family calls this "Double Advanced." He did not
get in last year and wants to qualify this year. In practice, the family understands it this way: a typical Advanced
Learning 5th grader takes the course called IMT1 next year, and a "Double Advanced" 5th grader takes IMT2
instead, skipping IMT1. To be ready, he needs to know the Grade 5 material solidly, all of Grade 6, and the rest
of IMT1 (the first three Grade 7 units), and ideally a first look at the start of IMT2.
The tutor. An older sibling (not a certified teacher) who teaches him on Saturdays and Sundays, with a parent
helping when needed. This has three consequences for you. First, every slide needs speaker notes with a short
script, expected student answers, and what to do when he is stuck. Second, every session needs a one-page run
sheet. Third, the materials must be easy to run without preparation.
The schedule. Ten weekends, starting Saturday, November 7, 2026, and ending Sunday, January 10, 2027. That
is 20 weekend sessions. Saturday sessions run about 75 minutes. Sunday sessions run 60 to 75 minutes. On each
weekday after a weekend (Monday to Friday) he does a practice packet that takes 15 to 20 minutes. Friday's
packet is a weekly check.
What "good" looks like. At the end of ten weeks he can explain and draw why fractions divide the way they
do, solve ratio and percent problems with tape diagrams and tables, handle decimals and long division
accurately, write and solve simple equations, work with negative numbers on a number line, find areas and
surface areas, and read and compute basic statistics. He can do this on problems he has not seen before. Speed
matters less than accuracy and explanation.
A caution from the district. BSD's own guidance says "Faster is NOT better" and warns against skipping
content. This program should therefore build real understanding with models, not teach shortcuts or tricks
without meaning. If you must choose between covering more topics and teaching a topic properly, teach it
properly.

Verified from BSD web pages (read in November 2026).

BSD's Advanced Learning math sequence for the typical Centered student, with one year of acceleration:
grade 2 studies Grade 3 standards, grade 3 studies Grade 4, grade 4 studies Grade 5 (IM Grade 5), grade 5

BSD says a student may "on occasion" be accelerated beyond one year, that such decisions are made at the
elementary school level only, and that they are "a data-based guidance team decision only." Parents may not
enroll a child in a course beyond the next one in the progression. IMT1 and IMT2 have no equivalent outside
courses and must be taken during the regular school year.
•
BSD says elementary math services are determined by "curriculum placement tests in grades 2-4." BSD has
not published the name, content, format, cutoff scores, or dates of these tests.
•
BSD's identification data for Advanced Learning include the i-Ready math diagnostic (taken three times a
year by K-5 students), state tests, and the CogAT. The published bar for math-domain identification includes
math achievement "2 years above grade level" or at or above the 98th percentile. A 4th grader at "2 years
above" is performing at Grade 6 level.
•
IMT1 is built from IM Grade 6 Units 1 to 8 plus IM Grade 7 Units 1 to 3. IMT2 is built from IM Grade 7
Units 4 to 8 plus IM Grade 8 Units 1, 2, 3, 7 and 8. (Details in Part 3.)
Unknown. What the placement evaluation contains, when it happens this year, who sits on the guidance team,

Standards framework. Washington is moving to revised 2026 math standards. Use Common Core State

Part 3. What to teach
3.1 Four layers of content, in priority order
Layer 1: Grade 5 foundations (must be solid). These are the IM Grade 5 units. He is working through them in
class now, and the family does not know exactly where his class is.
•
Finding volume (about 12 lessons): volume as unit cubes, length × width × height, base area × height. CCSS
5.MD.C.3 to 5.MD.C.5.
•
Fractions as quotients and fraction multiplication (17): a/b means a ÷ b, whole-number division with fraction
answers, multiplying a whole number or fraction by a fraction using area models. 5.NF.B.3, 5.NF.B.4.
•
Multiplying and dividing fractions (20): fraction × fraction, a unit fraction divided by a whole number, a
whole number divided by a unit fraction. 5.NF.B.4, 5.NF.B.6, 5.NF.B.7.
•
Multi-digit multiplication and division (21): the standard algorithm for multiplication, division of up to
4-digit numbers by 2-digit numbers. 5.NBT.B.5, 5.NBT.B.6.
•
Decimal place value and operations (26): decimals to thousandths, adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing
decimals to hundredths. 5.NBT.A.3, 5.NBT.A.4, 5.NBT.B.7.
•
More decimal and fraction operations (21): powers of 10, measurement conversion, adding and subtracting
fractions with unlike denominators, multiplication as scaling. 5.NBT.A.1, 5.NBT.A.2, 5.NF.A.1, 5.NF.A.2,
5.NF.B.5, 5.MD.A.1.
•
Shapes on the coordinate plane (13): plotting in the first quadrant, graphing number patterns. 5.G.A.1,
5.G.A.2, 5.OA.B.3.
Layer 2: Grade 6 (all eight units; this is IMT1 Units 1 to 8).
•
Area and surface area (19 lessons): areas of parallelograms and triangles, polygons by decomposing, nets,
surface area. 6.G.A.1, 6.G.A.4, 6.EE.A.1, 6.EE.A.2.
•
Introducing ratios (17): ratio as a relationship, equivalent ratios, discrete diagrams, double number lines,
tables, part-part-whole. 6.RP.A.1, 6.RP.A.3.
•
Unit rates and percentages (17): unit rates in both directions, comparing rates, percent as a rate per 100,
benchmark percents, finding the part, the whole, and the percent. 6.RP.A.2, 6.RP.A.3.
•
Dividing fractions (17): meaning of fraction division in equal-group, comparison, and area situations,
reciprocals, volume with fractional edges. 6.NS.A.1, 6.G.A.2.
•
Arithmetic in base ten (15, BSD calls it "Decimal Arithmetic"): decimals of any length, long division
including decimal quotients. 6.NS.B.2, 6.NS.B.3.
•
Expressions and equations (19): variables, exponents, equivalent expressions, the distributive property,
solving px = q and x + p = q, tables and graphs for two related quantities. 6.EE.A.1 to 6.EE.A.4, 6.EE.B.5 to
6.EE.B.7, 6.EE.C.9.
•
Rational numbers (19, BSD calls it "Positive and Negative Numbers"): negative numbers, opposites,
absolute value, inequalities, the four-quadrant coordinate plane, GCF and LCM. 6.NS.B.4, 6.NS.C.5 to
6.NS.C.8, 6.EE.B.8.
•
Data sets and distributions (18): statistical questions, dot plots, histograms, mean, median, mean absolute
deviation (MAD), interquartile range (IQR), box plots. 6.SP.A.1 to 6.SP.A.3, 6.SP.B.4, 6.SP.B.5.
Layer 3: Grade 7 Units 1 to 3 (the rest of IMT1).

3.2 Depth policy
Grade 6 plus Grade 7 Units 1 to 3 is about 180 IM lessons of class time. Ten weekends cannot teach all of that
at class depth. The program therefore teaches the core ideas of each unit well in two sessions and uses the daily
packets for repetition and spiral review. Say plainly in the Design Decisions note what each unit leaves out. As
a starting point, compress or leave out: building histograms from raw data with unusual bin widths, GCF and
LCM word problems beyond the basics, surface area of pyramids with non-square bases, complex fractions in
equations, repeating decimals, and the optional "Putting It All Together" units.

4.2 Session blueprints
Each blueprint gives the content you must teach, the structural cases the problems must cover from baseline to
hardest, the misconception the exit ticket must catch, and the exit ticket target. You choose the exact numbers
and contexts. The example items in the blueprints are suggestions and must be re-verified if you use them.
S1. Saturday, Nov 7. Starting line: diagnostic, then fractions on the number line
•
Timing. Diagnostic 35 minutes in two sittings with a 5-minute break. Review and talk-through 5 minutes.
Mini-lesson 25 minutes. Exit ticket 5 minutes. No separate retrieval warm-up this session.
•
Standards. 4.NF.A.1, 4.NF.A.2, 5.NF.A.1, 5.NF.B.3, 5.NBT.A.3.
•
Goals. Place fractions, mixed numbers, improper fractions, and decimals on one number line. Compare and
order fractions with unlike denominators using benchmarks (0, 1/2, 1) and common denominators. See a
fraction as division (a/b = a ÷ b).
•
Models. Number line, fraction strips, tape diagrams.
•
Cases, baseline to hardest. Same denominator. Same numerator. Benchmark comparison (3/8 and 1/2).
Neither shares a denominator or numerator (3/8 and 2/5). Improper fraction and mixed number on a number
line. Fraction versus decimal (5/8 and 0.65). A number between two whole numbers placed on a line scaled
in different units.

•
Misconception for the exit ticket. A bigger denominator means a bigger fraction.
•
Exit ticket target. Order 2/3, 5/8, and 0.65 and place them on a number line (answer 5/8, 0.65, 2/3). A
student who orders by denominator gets it wrong.
S2. Sunday, Nov 8. Multiplying and dividing with fractions: what the models show
•
Standards. 5.NF.B.3 to 5.NF.B.7.
•
Goals. Multiply a whole number and a fraction, and two fractions, using area models. Predict whether a
product is larger or smaller than a factor (multiplication as scaling). Divide a whole number by a unit
fraction and a unit fraction by a whole number with tape diagrams and stories. Explain "how many 1/3s are
in 2."
•
Cases. Whole number times fraction (4 × 2/3). Fraction times fraction (2/3 × 3/4). Fraction times mixed
number. Factor less than 1 (product smaller) and greater than 1 (product larger). Whole number divided by
unit fraction (4 ÷ 1/3 = 12). Unit fraction divided by whole number (1/3 ÷ 4 = 1/12). A story for each of the
last two, contrasting "how many in all" with "how big is each share."
•
Misconceptions. Multiplication always makes bigger. Division always makes smaller. Mixing up 4 ÷ 1/3
and 1/3 ÷ 4.
•
Exit ticket target. 4 ÷ 1/2 in a story ("How many half-pizzas can be made from 4 pizzas?" answer 8).
S3. Saturday, Nov 14. Ratios: the same relationship with different amounts
•
Standards. 6.RP.A.1, 6.RP.A.3a (start).
•
Goals. Describe a ratio with "for every" language and a : b notation using discrete diagrams and tape
diagrams. Make equivalent ratios by multiplying or dividing both quantities by the same number. Tell
additive change from multiplicative change.
•
Cases. Ratio read from a picture (3 red to 2 blue). Order matters (3 : 2 is not 2 : 3). Scale up by a whole
number. Scale down by dividing (12 : 8 to 3 : 2). A scale factor that is not a whole number (4 : 6 to 10 : ?).
Missing-value problems in recipes, mixtures, and groups.
•
Misconceptions. Additive reasoning (2 : 3 becomes 4 : 5 by adding 2). Reading 3 : 2 as "3 out of 2."
Reversing the order of the quantities.
•
Exit ticket target. "A mix has 5 red tiles for every 3 blue tiles. How many red tiles go with 12 blue tiles?"
(20; additive reasoning gives 14.)
S4. Sunday, Nov 15. Ratio tables, double number lines, and comparing ratios
•
Standards. 6.RP.A.1, 6.RP.A.3a, 6.RP.A.3b.
•
Goals. Build and read ratio tables and double number lines. Solve missing-value problems two ways.
Compare two ratios by finding a common multiple. Solve part-part-whole problems ("3 yellow to 2 blue
makes 20 liters"). Plot equivalent ratio pairs in the first quadrant and notice they lie on a line through the
origin.
•
Cases. Table with one missing value. The same problem solved two ways. Comparing 2 : 3 and 3 : 5.
Part-part-whole with a total given. Points from a ratio table plotted on a grid.
•
Misconceptions. Comparing ratios by subtracting. Confusing part-to-part with part-to-whole. Adding the
same amount down a table.

Exit ticket target. Mix A: 3 cups juice to 2 cups water. Mix B: 5 cups juice to 4 cups water. Which tastes

S5. Saturday, Nov 21. Rates and unit rates
•
Standards. 6.RP.A.2, 6.RP.A.3b.
•
Goals. Find a unit rate from a ratio in both directions (x per 1 y and y per 1 x). Interpret rates in context
(price per pound, miles per hour). Compare deals by unit rate. Use a unit rate to solve problems.
•
Cases. Whole-number unit rate (12 miles in 3 hours). Decimal unit rate ($4.50 for 6 oranges). Fraction unit
rate (2 cups for 3 servings). The same ratio expressed as two different unit rates. Comparing two deals with
different quantities. A rate where the time is not a whole number of hours (6 miles in 45 minutes gives 8
miles per hour). Constant-speed tables.
•
Misconceptions. Dividing in the wrong direction. Naming the wrong "per." Believing a unit rate must be a
whole number.
•
Exit ticket target. "A runner covers 6 miles in 45 minutes. How many miles per hour? How many minutes
per mile?" (8 miles per hour; 7.5 minutes per mile.) Do not require fraction division yet.
S6. Sunday, Nov 22. Percent as a rate per 100. Includes Mini Check A
•
Standards. 6.RP.A.3c.
•
Goals. Understand percent as a rate per 100. Use tape diagrams and double number lines for benchmark
percents (10%, 25%, 50%, 75%). Find the part, find the whole, and find the percent. Work with percents
above 100.
•
Cases. Percent of a whole number (30% of 80). Percent from part and whole (18 of 24). Find the whole (18
is 30% of what?). Percent above 100 (150% of 40). A non-benchmark percent (35%). A result that is a
decimal.
•
Misconceptions. Treating "18 is 30% of a number" as "30% of 18." Reading percent as a count. Thinking
percents cannot exceed 100.
•
Exit ticket target. "18 is 30% of a number. What is the number?" (60.)
•
Mini Check A. A 15-minute mixed check of 8 items covering Grade 5 fractions, ratios, rates, and percents.
Provide a printable version and a key.
S7. Saturday, Nov 28. What does it mean to divide by a fraction?
•
Standards. 6.NS.A.1.
•
Goals. Interpret a ÷ b as "how many groups of b fit in a." Use tape diagrams and number lines. Write a story
for a division expression. Compute quotients when the divisor is a unit fraction, then when it is not.
•
Cases. Whole number divided by unit fraction (3 ÷ 1/4 = 12). Whole number divided by a non-unit fraction
(6 ÷ 3/4 = 8). Fraction divided by fraction with a whole-number answer (2/3 ÷ 1/6 = 4). Fraction divided by
fraction with a non-whole answer (3/4 ÷ 1/2 = 1 1/2). A quotient less than 1 (1/2 ÷ 3/4 = 2/3). The "how
much for one whole group" reading (2/3 cup fills 3/4 of a jar; how much fills the whole jar? 8/9). A
story-writing task.
•
Misconceptions. Dividing in the wrong order. Multiplying instead. "Division always makes smaller."
•
Exit ticket target. 1/2 ÷ 3/4 with a diagram (2/3).

S8. Sunday, Nov 29. Dividing fractions: the reciprocal and its uses
•
Standards. 6.NS.A.1, 6.G.A.2.
•
Goals. See from models and patterns that dividing by a/b gives the same result as multiplying by b/a.
Compute quotients with fractions and mixed numbers. Solve problems: equal groups, pieces of a given
length, a missing side of a rectangle, the volume of a box with fractional edges.
•
Cases. Fraction divided by whole number (2/3 ÷ 4). Whole number divided by fraction (5 ÷ 2/3 = 7 1/2).
Fraction divided by fraction. Mixed number divided by fraction (2 1/4 ÷ 3/4 = 3). Cutting a length into
pieces. A rectangle with area 3/4 square foot and width 1/2 foot (length 1 1/2 feet). A prism with edges 1/2,
3/4, and 2 feet (volume 3/4 cubic foot).
•
Misconceptions. Inverting the wrong number. Forgetting to convert mixed numbers. Multiplying when
asked to divide.
•
Exit ticket target. "A ribbon 2 1/4 yards long is cut into pieces that are each 3/8 yard long. How many
pieces?" (6.)
S9. Saturday, Dec 5. Decimals of any length: adding, subtracting, multiplying
•
Standards. 6.NS.B.3, 5.NBT.B.7.
•
Goals. Use place-value diagrams. Line up decimal points when adding and subtracting. Multiply decimals
by reasoning from the whole-number product and an estimate. Check answers for reasonableness.
•
Cases. Adding decimals of different lengths (3.5 + 12.075). Subtracting across zeros (10 − 3.472). Decimal
times whole number. Decimal times decimal with a product smaller than both factors (0.4 × 0.3 = 0.12).
Products that need leading zeros (0.25 × 0.04 = 0.01). Money contexts.
•
Misconceptions. Aligning digits at the right edge instead of the decimal point. Misplacing the decimal point
in a product. "Multiplying always makes bigger."
•
Exit ticket target. 0.35 × 0.04 (0.014).
S10. Sunday, Dec 6. Long division and dividing with decimals
•
Standards. 6.NS.B.2, 6.NS.B.3.
•
Goals. Use the standard long-division algorithm fluently. Continue a division into decimal places. Divide a
decimal by a decimal by scaling both numbers by the same power of 10. Estimate and check by multiplying.
•
Cases. Whole number quotient from a 3-digit by 2-digit division. Whole number divided by whole number
with a decimal quotient (45 ÷ 8 = 5.625). Decimal divided by whole number (7.2 ÷ 6 = 1.2). Decimal
divided by decimal (7.2 ÷ 0.06 = 120). Quotient less than 1 (3 ÷ 12 = 0.25). A divisor less than 1 producing a
quotient larger than the dividend. Avoid repeating decimals.
•
Misconceptions. Moving the decimal point in only one number. Misplacing the decimal point in the
quotient. Stopping when a remainder is not zero. "The quotient must be smaller than the dividend."
•
Exit ticket target. 7.2 ÷ 0.06 (120).

S11. Saturday, Dec 12. Expressions: variables, exponents, equivalent expressions
•
Standards. 6.EE.A.1 to 6.EE.A.4, 6.EE.B.6.
•
Goals. Write expressions from stories and diagrams. Evaluate expressions. Understand whole-number
exponents and order of operations. Build equivalent expressions with the distributive property using area
diagrams, and by combining like terms. Test equivalence by substituting numbers.
•
Cases. Expression from a story (x dollars each for 4 items). Evaluating for a given value. Exponents (3²
compared with 3 × 2; 2³; (1/2)²). Distributive property with an area diagram (3(x + 4) = 3x + 12). Combining
like terms (x + x + x = 3x). Telling equivalent from not equivalent (3(x + 4) and 3x + 4).
•
Misconceptions. A variable stands for one particular number. 3² = 6. 3(x + 4) = 3x + 4. x + x = x².
•
Exit ticket target. Which expressions are equivalent to 2(3x + 5)? Offer 6x + 10, 6x + 5, 5x + 7, and 6x + 7.
(6x + 10 only; 6x + 5 is the common error.)
S12. Sunday, Dec 13. Equations: tape diagrams, hanger diagrams, and solving
•
Standards. 6.EE.B.5 to 6.EE.B.7, 6.EE.C.9.
•
Goals. Understand a solution as a value that makes an equation true. Solve x + p = q and px = q with tape
diagrams and hanger diagrams. Solve with fractions and decimals. Write an equation for a story. Connect a
table, an equation, and a graph for two related quantities, and name the independent and dependent variable.
•
Cases. x + p = q with whole numbers. px = q with whole numbers (4x = 36). With decimals (1.5x = 6.3, x =
4.2). With fractions ((3/4)x = 9, x = 12). A story that leads to an equation. A table of values from an equation
(y = 3x) and its graph.
•
Misconceptions. Doing different operations to the two sides. Solving px = q by subtracting p. Reading 3x as
3 + x. Solving (3/4)x = 9 by multiplying 9 by 3/4.
•
Exit ticket target. Solve (3/4)x = 9 (12).
S13. Saturday, Dec 19. Area of parallelograms and triangles
•
Standards. 6.G.A.1.
•
Goals. Derive the area formulas by cutting and rearranging. Identify a base and its matching height. See a
triangle as half a parallelogram. Compute areas when the height is not a side.
•
Cases. Rectangle area review. A parallelogram with base 8 cm, slanted side 5 cm, height 4 cm (32 square
cm). A right triangle with legs 3 and 4. A triangle whose height is not a side (sides 13, 14, and 15 cm, with
base 14 and height 12, area 84 square cm). A triangle drawn so the height falls outside it (obtuse). The same
triangle with a different side chosen as the base, giving the same area. Geometry check: any triangle you
draw with a stated height must be geometrically possible, so verify the numbers in code with the
Pythagorean theorem before using them.
•
Misconceptions. Multiplying the base by the slanted side. Leaving out the 1/2 in a triangle. A height that is
not perpendicular. Confusing area with perimeter. Wrong units.
•
Exit ticket target. A triangle with sides 5, 5, and 6 cm, base 6 cm, and height 4 cm (area 12 square cm). A
student who uses the slanted side 5 as the height gets 15.
